## Technical explanation (300 words)

HumanFirst-Route separates the decision about **when and how much to assist** from the production of the assistance text. The project’s reinforcement-learning policy operates on observable learner and task state, such as the reasoning operation, estimated knowledge, difficulty, attempts, help-seeking and frustration. It selects a route and an assistance level under operation-specific limits. The policy does not write natural-language hints. This separation allows the policy’s allocation decisions to be evaluated independently from the language model that realizes a selected level of help.

For the P4 language-generation comparison, Qwen3-0.6B serves as the frozen base generator and as the initialization for a low-rank adaptation (LoRA). LoRA adds trainable low-rank matrices to selected transformer projections while keeping the underlying model weights frozen. In this experiment the adapter was trained for three epochs on 2,822 filtered hint examples. Most retained targets were generated by the same base model and passed the project’s existing rule checks; a small remainder was handwritten. Thus, the fine-tuning is best described as filtered self-distillation, not training on a new independent source of expert judgments.

At inference, a caller can supply the task context, reasoning operation and requested assistance level, and the selected generator produces a candidate hint. A separate rule-based validator then checks a limited set of constraints, including level compliance and detectable answer leakage. It can reject, regenerate or route a candidate to a safer fallback in the broader assistance pipeline. This validator is intentionally not presented as a judge of truth or teaching value: it does not reliably determine whether a hint is factually correct, relevant to the operation, useful, or beneficial to learning.

The paired experiment compares the base model and its LoRA adapter on identical held-out prompts with the same decoding configuration and per-example seeds. Its outcomes characterize first-pass validator acceptance and generation latency. They do not measure the reinforcement-learning policy, the policy-generator interaction end to end, or learner transfer. Therefore, the experiment informs the generator-and-validator component of the architecture, while the policy remains a distinct decision-making component requiring its own end-to-end evaluation.

## Results (183 words)

On 618 paired held-out outputs from 41 task clusters, the base Qwen3-0.6B model achieved 99.19% acceptance under the project’s rule-based validator, while the LoRA-adapted model achieved 99.51%. The paired difference was +0.32 percentage points. A task-cluster bootstrap gave a 95% interval from −0.73 to +1.27 percentage points, which includes zero; this evaluation therefore does not establish a reliable improvement in validator acceptance. Both rates were close to the validator ceiling.

Mean generation latency was 1.53 seconds for the base model and 2.62 seconds for LoRA, approximately 72% higher for the adapted model in this run. Descriptively, coding acceptance was 98.51% versus 98.88%, and science acceptance was 99.71% versus 100.00%. Across assistance levels, acceptance was equal at levels 1 and 3, and numerically higher by 0.65 percentage points for LoRA at levels 2 and 4. These strata are descriptive and do not provide separate confirmatory evidence.

Acceptance means passing the encoded compliance checks without a detected leakage flag. It is not a measure of hint quality. In particular, these results provide no statistically established evidence of better factual accuracy, pedagogical value, or learning outcomes. The training targets were largely filtered outputs from the same base model, so the comparison should be interpreted as a narrow self-distillation experiment.

## Limitations

1. **Self-distillation:** the training targets were mostly filtered base-model generations, not independent expert or human annotations. Fine-tuning may reproduce the base model’s patterns and errors.
2. **Validator ceiling and construct limits:** base acceptance was already 99.19%. The rule validator checks only encoded patterns; it can miss semantic errors and can produce false positives. A pass is not evidence of factual correctness, operation relevance or pedagogical usefulness.
3. **No human evaluation:** independent human ratings were not conducted. Qualitative examples in `results/qwen3_06b_lora_v1/ERROR_ANALYSIS.md` are unblinded inspection, not human-verified results.
4. **No demonstrated end-to-end transfer effect:** this experiment did not evaluate policy decisions coupled to generated hints in sessions with learners, simulated or real. It provides no evidence that LoRA improves unaided transfer or real learning.
5. **Latency and scope:** the LoRA outputs were slower on average in this run. Results concern one pinned model revision, one adapter, the selected held-out set and first-pass generations.
